What Grand Theft Auto VI means for Scotland’s games industry and future developers

By Sean Friel 22nd April 2026

The most profitable entertainment product of all time, Grand Theft Auto V, was primarily developed by Rockstar North in Edinburgh, placing Scotland at the centre of the gaming industry. Thirteen years on, anticipation for its successor has reached unprecedented levels bringing both excitement and disruption across the wider games industry.

Behind the hype surrounding GTA VI lies growing unease across the games industry, alongside increasing scrutiny of the working conditions behind game development. As Scotland’s video games sector gains global recognition, these developments raise deeper questions about labour, rights, and the future of work within one of its most successful industries. While Rockstar North represents one of Scotland’s most visible success stories, its dominance also exposes structural issues within the wider games industry.

Working conditions within the games industry are often overlooked in mainstream coverage, where attention is typically focused on the quality of games and, in Rockstar’s case, controversy surrounding in-game content. However, far less attention is given to the labour practices and working conditions that sustain that success. In October 2025, Rockstar Games dismissed 31 employees from its Edinburgh studio over inconsequential private messages between staff, prompting accusations of union busting from the Independent Workers' Union of Great Britain. Scotland’s global reputation in gaming will continue to be defined by prestige and scale, however this masks an industry that remains uneven, pressured, and in need of greater scrutiny.

Scotland has a strong concentration of studios and emerging talent, supported by institutions such as Abertay University in Dundee, which ranked top international school for video game design. The sector includes more than 160 active studios, supporting over 2,000 jobs and contributing approximately £188.5 million annually to the economy. The success of the Grand Theft Auto series has positioned Scotland as a global hub for game development, attracting investment, talent, and opportunity. For many aspiring developers, Rockstar represents a benchmark of creative ambition and career potential. However, recognising this success should not prevent a more critical examination of how it is sustained, and who ultimately benefits.

Jasper Stocker, a freelance developer claims “The games industry has been devastated by relentless layoffs over the past few years, with thousands of talented developers—many of them Scottish—losing their jobs. Freelancers and small studios are feeling this even more acutely, as the ecosystem we depend on contracts around us.” This prominence highlights a growing imbalance. Large, globally backed studios operate with vast resources, long development cycles, and significant marketing reach, while smaller developers often face limited funding and far less visibility. Many rely on short-term contracts, freelance work, or external publishing deals, creating instability that contrasts with the perceived security of major studios. This has been intensified by widespread layoffs across the global games industry, with thousands of developers, including those in Scotland, affected.

The influence of titles like GTA VI also shapes expectations across the sector. Production costs of the title are expected to reach $3 billion, games of this scale set technical and creative benchmarks that are often unattainable for smaller teams, yet still define audience demand. For emerging developers in Scotland, this creates pressure to meet standards set by companies like Rockstar North without comparable resources. At the same time, reports of restructuring and intense production cycles highlight the instability behind these high-profile releases.

Media coverage plays a role in maintaining this imbalance. Attention is overwhelmingly directed towards major releases and established studios, with outlets prioritising access and audience demand. Gaming publications such IGN frequently centre coverage on GTA VI, often at the expense of smaller developers, this creates a cycle in which large studios dominate both production and representation. While developers with small budgets have achieved major success with breakout titles such as Dundee-based 4J Studios’ console versions of Minecraft, these remain relatively rare success stories, and most struggle for visibility. Journalism not only reflects the industry but also helps shape which stories and which issues are brought to the forefront.

As anticipation for GTA VI continues to build, so too should scrutiny of the industry it represents. Scotland’s position at the centre of global gaming is a clear success story, but one shaped by tensions around labour, visibility, and sustainability. If GTA VI is set to redefine expectations once again, it should also prompt a broader conversation about what kind of industry Scotland wants to support, and whether future success can balance global prestige with fair and inclusive working conditions.
